Reciprocal wave source on the linked normal geometry

Updated September 14, 2026

conditional calculation

The bounded connected carrier gives a concrete spatial frequency operator. Its occupied wave also supplies a reciprocal source for the supporting texture and connection. Computing both sources reveals a useful common response and a remaining dynamical constraint: the wave drives a genuine deformation along which the proposed geometric functional is constant. Thus solving the geometry without its occupied wave does not yet give a stationary particle.

The calculation concerns the linked normal operator on an ordinary unbounded plane. All spinor and canonical-pair components are retained. The coefficients have not been assigned an independent physical interaction field or a physical energy separate from the one IDWT field. The forces below are model derivatives in the stated units, not measured couplings or encounter rates.

Deform the linked geometry itself

The full planar geometric rule can be written with a holomorphic polynomial P and a real function u:

W=e−uP(z),A=∇⊥u,Δu=2(1−e−2u∣P∣2).(4) W=e^{-u}P(z),\qquad \boldsymbol A=\nabla^\perp u, \qquad \Delta u=2(1-e^{-2u}|P|^2). \tag{4} W=e−uP(z),A=∇⊥u,Δu=2(1−e−2u∣P∣2).(4)

The radial reference has P=z^n and a=r u'. Move along this same rule with P=z^n+epsilon z^(n-k), where 1<=k<=n. For real epsilon, write delta u=f_k(r) cos(k theta). Linearizing (4) gives

[−∂r2−r−1∂r+k2/r2+4w2]fk=4w2r−k.(5) \boxed{ \left[-\partial_r^2-r^{-1}\partial_r+k^2/r^2+4w^2\right]f_k =4w^2 r^{-k}.} \tag{5} [−∂r2​−r−1∂r​+k2/r2+4w2]fk​=4w2r−k.​(5)

The solution is regular as r^k at zero and approaches r^(-k) at infinity. Its associated linked variations are

δW=w[(r−k−fk/2)ei(n−k)θ−(fk/2)ei(n+k)θ],δAr=kfksin⁡(kθ)/r,δAθ=fk′cos⁡(kθ).(6) \begin{aligned} \delta W&=w\left[(r^{-k}-f_k/2)e^{i(n-k)\theta} -(f_k/2)e^{i(n+k)\theta}\right],\\ \delta A_r&=k f_k\sin(k\theta)/r,\qquad \delta A_\theta=f_k'\cos(k\theta). \end{aligned} \tag{6} δWδAr​​=w[(r−k−fk​/2)ei(n−k)θ−(fk​/2)ei(n+k)θ],=kfk​sin(kθ)/r,δAθ​=fk′​cos(kθ).​(6)

For a purely imaginary epsilon replace the cosine by a sine and retain the corresponding complex coefficients. This preserves the full phase information. For k>=2 the center is unchanged, and the explicit polarization weight varies through delta a=r delta A_theta.

The k=1 case is an independent control. Equation (5) has the exact solution f_1=a/(n r). If the center and h move too, (6) is a common translation. The complete frequency derivative then vanishes. Numerical refinement verifies that cancellation. Fourier contraction is used directly for the smallest responses; subtracting large angular samples would otherwise obscure the translation check through roundoff.

For arbitrary zeros of P the same first-order geometric rule has cost G=2 pi n. Moving those zeros therefore supplies genuine constant-cost directions. It preserves the complete normal gap and kernel count, but need not preserve the projected particle frequency.

One spatial endpoint coefficient gives the response grouping

For k=n the polynomial changes by a constant. Angular selection now makes the projected variation especially simple. The polarization variation has angular difference n, larger than the largest difference n-1 between retained labels, and gives no projected entry. The variation of the degree-one multiplier h can reach only the opposite endpoints:

δT=γ κnϵ ∣0⟩⟨n−1∣.(7) \boxed{\delta T=\gamma\,\kappa_n\epsilon\, |0\rangle\langle n-1|.} \tag{7} δT=γκn​ϵ∣0⟩⟨n−1∣.​(7)

Here kappa_n is a real spatial integral, independent of beta or any particle frequency. It is not the binding coefficient in the separate polarization-core action.

There is an explicit independent formula for this integral. Let f=f_n, U=U_0, V=V_0 and define

b0=w(r−n−f/2)U+14(nf/r+f′)V,b1=14(nf/r−f′)U+12wfV.(8) \begin{aligned} b_0&=w(r^{-n}-f/2)U+\tfrac14(nf/r+f')V,\\ b_1&=\tfrac14(nf/r-f')U+\tfrac12wfV. \end{aligned} \tag{8} b0​b1​​=w(r−n−f/2)U+41​(nf/r+f′)V,=41​(nf/r−f′)U+21​wfV.​(8)

Let (Y_0,Y_1) be the two positive-chiral radial components of D^(-1)(I-P) h Phi_(n-1), with their known angular factors removed. They are computed from (2). Reflection between the endpoint modes gives

κn=−4π∫0∞r(b0Y0+b1Y1) dr.(9) \boxed{\kappa_n=-4\pi\int_0^\infty r(b_0Y_0+b_1Y_1)\,dr.} \tag{9} κn​=−4π∫0∞​r(b0​Y0​+b1​Y1​)dr.​(9)

For a jointly normalized pair (u,v), the complete projected response is

∂ω∂ϵ=2γκnRe⁡(v0∗un−1).(10) \boxed{\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\epsilon} =2\gamma\kappa_n\operatorname{Re}(v_0^*u_{n-1}).} \tag{10} ∂ϵ∂ω​=2γκn​Re(v0∗​un−1​).​(10)

Both vectors have norm approaching 1/sqrt(2) on the small-frequency branch. Their endpoint product tends to (-1)^(n-1)/2, independently of beta. Thus different masses within one depth approach a common absolute shape response by the same spatial rule. No division by mass or separately chosen particle coefficient produces this grouping.

The computed geometric derivatives are approximately -0.0802981 at n=5, -0.0339063 at n=6 and -0.000529886 at n=10. These are defined in the same chosen dimensionless coefficient convention; they do not derive physical relative sector scales or neutrino suppression.

At n=6 and gamma=10000, the independently refined shape derivatives are about 339.061284 for beta=15.5 and 339.053384 for beta=37.5. The frequencies differ by approximately the assigned simplex ratio, while this response differs by about 2.33e-5 in relative terms. The sign follows the retained phase and endpoint convention. It is not a sign assigned to inertia.

The force in (10) is nonzero along the constant-cost geometric direction. Therefore an additive geometric functional alone does not hold the occupied radial configuration stationary. The wave and sustaining motion must be solved together, or a different explicit common geometric rule must be tested. The near-equal response is useful evidence about this candidate operator, not a completed physical particle-pair coupling.
