Bilateral polarization coupling

Updated September 10, 2026

Summary: Keeping both objects polarized gives covariant tensor pairings, distinct source and response zeros, and a reciprocal orientation model with exactly conserved relative overlap.

Both objects carry spatial polarization

Let the two particle patterns carry spatial tensors Ja(x;α)J_a(x;\alpha)Ja​(x;α) and Jb(y;β)J_b(y;\beta)Jb​(y;β), where α,β\alpha,\betaα,β label their respective preparations. These parameters describe fields on actual nested macroscopic spatial submanifolds of one infinite manifold. They are not additional physical dimensions. Each object has its own polarization and can change in response to the other; a fixed-target calculation is a conditional snapshot.

The working physical hypothesis is that this reciprocal motion can improve or worsen the match between the spatial patterns. An encounter may therefore strengthen its subsequent coupling or move the patterns toward incompatibility. Changing compatibility is a proposed part of the mechanism, to be tested from the same field equations.

The calculation must follow both patterns during the encounter and determine which responses initiate and sustain that change. Initial overlap alone does not determine the outcome. Neither automatic alignment nor conserved relative overlap is a general requirement; the exactly conserved overlap in the benchmark below is a property of its specified restricted dynamics.

A specified reciprocal two-form term can be written

U[α,β]=−gRe⁡∫ ⁣∫⟨Ja(x;α),Kab(x,y)Jb(y;β)⟩dμa(x)dμb(y).(1) U[\alpha,\beta] =-g\operatorname{Re}\int\!\int \left\langle J_a(x;\alpha), \mathcal K_{ab}(x,y)J_b(y;\beta)\right\rangle d\mu_a(x)d\mu_b(y). \tag{1} U[α,β]=−gRe∫∫⟨Ja​(x;α),Kab​(x,y)Jb​(y;β)⟩dμa​(x)dμb​(y).(1)

The kernel includes the actual spatial maps, frame transport, and any derivatives prescribed by the action. All tensors must be paired in compatible frames. Reciprocity uses the adjoint kernel for the reverse variation. Equation (1) is a conditional term of the one field's energy; the kernel and tensor observables must come from its action.

Under a common rotation of all physical geometric data, J↦RJRTJ\mapsto RJR^TJ↦RJRT and K\mathcal KK transforms correspondingly, leaving (1) unchanged. Rotating only the particles while keeping an anisotropic environment fixed is a different physical preparation and need not preserve the energy.

For a local isotropic benchmark, with two real tensors at one common point, the expression reduces to

U=−g⟨A,B⟩,⟨A,B⟩=−12tr⁡(AB).(2) U=-g\langle A,B\rangle, \qquad\langle A,B\rangle=-\frac12\operatorname{tr}(AB). \tag{2} U=−g⟨A,B⟩,⟨A,B⟩=−21​tr(AB).(2)

A zero static source need not be a zero response

U(a,β)=−g a s(β),s(β)=⟨Bγ,Jb(β)⟩(6) U(a,\beta)=-g\,a\,s(\beta), \qquad s(\beta)=\langle B_\gamma,J_b(\beta)\rangle \tag{6} U(a,β)=−gas(β),s(β)=⟨Bγ​,Jb​(β)⟩(6)

for one real wave quadrature. The background can satisfy s(β0)=0s(\beta_0)=0s(β0​)=0 while the mixed derivative is nonzero:

∂2U∂a ∂β=−g⟨Bγ,∂Jb∂β⟩.(7) \boxed{\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial a\,\partial\beta} =-g\left\langle B_\gamma, \frac{\partial J_b}{\partial\beta}\right\rangle.} \tag{7} ∂a∂β∂2U​=−g⟨Bγ​,∂β∂Jb​​⟩.​(7)

Jb(θ)=cos⁡θ (e2∧e3)+sin⁡θ (e1∧e2). J_b(\theta) =\cos\theta\,(e_2\wedge e_3) +\sin\theta\,(e_1\wedge e_2). Jb​(θ)=cosθ(e2​∧e3​)+sinθ(e1​∧e2​).

A solvable reciprocal orientation benchmark

For the finite isotropic energy (2), give AAA and BBB fixed coadjoint orbits of SO(D)SO(D)SO(D) and positive symplectic weights ℓa,ℓb\ell_a,\ell_bℓa​,ℓb​. Specify the Lie–Poisson convention

ℓaA˙=[A,∇AU],ℓbB˙=[B,∇BU]. \ell_a\dot A=[A,\nabla_AU], \qquad \ell_b\dot B=[B,\nabla_BU]. ℓa​A˙=[A,∇A​U],ℓb​B˙=[B,∇B​U].

The equations are

ℓaA˙=−g[A,B],ℓbB˙=g[A,B].(9) \ell_a\dot A=-g[A,B], \qquad \ell_b\dot B=g[A,B]. \tag{9} ℓa​A˙=−g[A,B],ℓb​B˙=g[A,B].(9)

Both polarizations respond. Their weighted sum S=ℓaA+ℓbBS=\ell_aA+\ell_bBS=ℓa​A+ℓb​B is constant, as are their individual spectra, the overlap ⟨A,B⟩\langle A,B\rangle⟨A,B⟩, and the energy. Since

A˙=gℓaℓb[S,A],B˙=gℓaℓb[S,B], \dot A=\frac{g}{\ell_a\ell_b}[S,A], \qquad \dot B=\frac{g}{\ell_a\ell_b}[S,B], A˙=ℓa​ℓb​g​[S,A],B˙=ℓa​ℓb​g​[S,B],

the exact solution is

R(t)=exp⁡(gtℓaℓbS),A(t)=R(t)A(0)R(t)T,B(t)=R(t)B(0)R(t)T.(10) \boxed{ R(t)=\exp\left(\frac{gt}{\ell_a\ell_b}S\right), \qquad A(t)=R(t)A(0)R(t)^T, \qquad B(t)=R(t)B(0)R(t)^T.} \tag{10} R(t)=exp(ℓa​ℓb​gt​S),A(t)=R(t)A(0)R(t)T,B(t)=R(t)B(0)R(t)T.​(10)

The weights are coefficients of a specified first-order orientation action, not values inferred from spatial depth. These orbit dynamics preserve tensor spectra and rotate both objects together. They produce precession, with no attraction toward a better matched relative angle. Testing the proposed improvement or loss of compatibility during an encounter therefore requires derived spatial motion, anisotropy, or response structure beyond this benchmark. The direction of that change must follow from the equations, rather than a rule that always favors alignment.

Equation (9) assumes the displayed orbit variations are admissible. Fixed sector embeddings or geometric constraints can restrict the allowed generators and require reaction terms. The benchmark does not establish that arbitrary rotations preserve every global nested embedding.

Following the full field beyond its mean rotation tensor

The coadjoint benchmark follows the two-form observables. They need not specify the complete polarized field at a higher spatial depth. Equal-current spatial polarizations derives a reciprocal six-inside-ten example from a rotation-moment energy and both full spatial kinetic terms. Its mean tensors remain fixed while the higher field can rotate into an orthogonal polarization. Thus even a two-form interaction can require more field information to describe its response than the mean two-forms alone retain.

Relation to the photon and higher-sector calculations

The tensor JdJ_dJd​ in photon plane coupling already specifies one target polarization, while E,k,cE,k,cE,k,c specify the photon's polarization and propagation. Its projector calculation freezes the target at that preparation. Equations (7)–(8) supply the additional test when both can respond.

The descending source starts from the higher pattern; its derivative gives the reverse response. The Dirac source derivative computes that derivative explicitly for one written nonlinear term. These descriptions can coexist: the higher geometry may determine the source while the two objects' relative polarizations determine which response is engaged.
