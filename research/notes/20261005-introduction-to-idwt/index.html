An Introduction to Infinite Dimensional Wave Theory

Updated November 5, 2026

Why do particles fall into families with similar properties but very different masses? Infinite Dimensional Wave Theory, or IDWT, investigates whether their physical dimensionality helps explain both features.

The proposal is one field in a shared higher-dimensional physical space, with particles confined to layers of different dimensionality. Physical spacetime has infinitely many ordinary spatial dimensions plus time. Matter occupies up to ten spatial dimensions; all remaining spatial directions are unbound for every particle. Our familiar three-dimensional view does not exhaust that space. The proposed electron has six spatial dimensions and the tau ten. All those directions are physical spatial directions of the same kind as the familiar three, without compactification.

The layers can be described as nested submanifolds of the same space. An electron is lower-dimensional relative to a tau, but is not a constituent inside it. The current containing-space hypothesis is flat. The physical law selecting and sustaining the observed particle patterns remains unfinished. Explicit candidate actions now realize some localization and motion requirements under stated premises; the layer picture alone is not that mechanism.

The new case for particle geometry and sector depths is the main research article. It connects the mass comparisons to dimensional criteria and explicit higher-dimensional constructions, with detailed derivations linked throughout. The dimensions remain proposed assignments, not experimentally measured extra spatial directions.

One Classification Across Several Questions

“Depth” means the dimensionality involved in a particle's bound structure. It is used in the mass rule, family assignments and proposed spin, charge and orbit constructions. Under exact uniform extension, translation along a pattern's own free directions changes nothing. Its center moves where its profile varies; changing its orientation is a separate physical operation.
