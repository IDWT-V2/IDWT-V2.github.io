An Introduction to Infinite Dimensional Wave Theory

Updated October 5, 2026

Why do particles fall into families with similar properties but very different masses? Infinite Dimensional Wave Theory, or IDWT, investigates whether their spatial wave geometry can explain both features.

The proposal is that particles are organized patterns of one field, associated with different spatial depths in a single nested manifold. The same depth is used throughout: it sets the shape of the combinatorial mass ladder, organizes the proposed particle families, and enters the orbit, spin, handedness and charge constructions. These are connected uses of one sector structure. The aim is to determine whether one physical law can produce all of them together.

The reason to investigate IDWT is a restrictive numerical pattern together with explicit mathematics that can be tested. It is an unfinished research program. Its spatial sectors are proposed assignments, not experimentally measured extra dimensions.

The Starting Idea

A familiar resonator can sustain several frequencies while retaining the same material structure. IDWT asks whether particle families could have a related explanation: different excitations of a shared kind of spatial structure, sustained by the surrounding field and its response.

Here spatial depth means ordinary spatial dimensions. The proposed electron structure has six; the tau has ten. Three electron directions are shared with an ordinary observer, but all six are the same kind of spatial direction. The dimensions are not compactified or treated as internal particle labels. Sectors nest; individual particles remain separate patterns. An electron is not a constituent of a tau.

The current fundamental-field proposal uses Dirac spinors, but the research also tests other effective kinetic descriptions. A shared action must ultimately explain localization, frequency, interaction and complete energy–momentum response. The analogy with a resonator motivates that work; it does not supply those equations by itself.

A Mass Pattern With Large Gaps Between Its Rungs

Mass can be expressed as a rest-energy frequency:

ωm=mc2ℏ. \omega_m=\frac{mc^2}{\hbar}. ωm​=ℏmc2​.

IDWT proposes

m(n,d)=μdS(n,d),S(n,d)=(n+d−1d). m(n,d)=\mu_d S(n,d),\qquad S(n,d)=\binom{n+d-1}{d}. m(n,d)=μd​S(n,d),S(n,d)=(dn+d−1​).

The positive integer nnn labels an excitation, ddd is its assigned spatial depth, and μd\mu_dμd​ is shared by particles at that depth. The count SSS is the number of ways to distribute up to n−1n-1n−1 excitations among ddd directions. It follows from stars and bars and the Hockey-Stick Identity. The count is exact mathematics; its identification with a physical frequency multiplier is the hypothesis to explain dynamically.

For the electron and muon, the maintained assignments give

S(13,6)=18564,S(35,6)=3838380,mμme=351517. S(13,6)=18564,\qquad S(35,6)=3838380, \qquad \frac{m_\mu}{m_e}=\frac{3515}{17}. S(13,6)=18564,S(35,6)=3838380,me​mμ​​=173515​.

The common scale cancels. This ratio lies about 17.3 parts per million below the measured benchmark ratio. It is close, but the discrepancy exceeds measurement uncertainty: the uncorrected expression is not an exact mass law.

Its rigidity makes the comparison interesting. At the muon assignment, moving down one rung changes the count by 15%; moving up changes it by approximately 17.14%. Adjusting the shared scale moves both particles. There is no small continuous adjustment of the integer count that repairs only the muon. The electron is a higher excitation too, not the sector's fundamental frequency.

The grouping and mass law use the same ddd, rather than assigning one depth to masses and a different label to particle families. Keep the Family Grouping in Alternative Tests

The maintained arrangement groups the particle families as shown above. An attempt to rearrange depths or sector scales must preserve those groupings: move grouped family members together, rather than assigning each particle its own best-fitting depth. The electron and muon stay together; the tau's explicit separate-depth assignment and shared charged-lepton scale relation must also be accounted for. Different assigned groups require distinct depths in the complete arrangement.

All groups must be tested simultaneously with fixed measured masses and one stated scale prescription. Moving one family into another family's depth requires finding a viable placement for the displaced family too. A scale change must follow the same declared rule across the arrangement; independently retuning each particle removes the constraint being tested. A comparison that relaxes family grouping or changes the scale law can be informative, but it tests a different hypothesis and must be labelled that way.

A stronger assignment study also tests complete rearrangements under the specified absolute scale prescription. Within that constrained model, only the maintained arrangement reaches every existing individual mass accuracy, even allowing all positive integer indices. That is a conditional uniqueness result for the stated prescription, not uniqueness among every possible physical theory. The indices and scale prescription have a history of development using known masses; their origin remains part of the problem.

What Else Does the Geometry Constrain

The electron orbit uses the same depth six favored by the electron–muon mass comparison. There is no separately chosen orbital depth. The six-dimensional electron proposal uses the geometry of CP3\mathbb{CP}^3CP3. Under a stated Hamiltonian preparation and observer map, its projected curves have a reproducible sequence of radial lobes. For angular label LLL, the construction gives 2L2L2L lobes across a specified class of monotone detector responses. This is a concrete geometric result. Connecting it to physical electron motion and atomic observations requires the detector coupling and a supported electron–nucleus solution. Orbit and observer map.

An explicit nonlinear Dirac profile supplies another test: its bound-sector norm converges at depths greater than two and diverges logarithmically at depth two. The complete profile family transforms with spin one-half. That fits the matter/boson depth division for this branch, under the stated finite-norm criterion. Spin-half transformation is supplied by the spinor premise; the additional result is the dimension-dependent profile threshold. Scalar and vector quadratic channels are available, but their existence alone does not derive the Higgs, W or Z as physical particles. Spin calculation.

The depth-five proposal supplies a further connection. Relative to an observer's three spatial directions, it leaves one two-dimensional normal plane. A specified normal texture can support a selected handed mode; a candidate conserved geometric generator also makes that mode neutral. This brings handedness, neutrality and spin-half covariance into one calculation, relevant to the neutrino assignment already tested by the mass-splitting ratio. Texture selection and the generator's identification with electric charge remain conditional. The construction does not yet derive the observed neutrino interaction rate or a complete massive-neutrino helicity law. Charge and handedness bridge.

At depth two, a transverse plane has two circular polarization weights, +1+1+1 and −1-1−1, matching the photon's helicity classes under the specified transverse-wave construction. The same assigned depth appears in the massive-boson mass ladder, although the massless photon and the scalar Higgs require distinct dynamical branches. A two-plane alone does not supply the full electromagnetic field equation or massive-vector polarizations. Photon geometry.

This is the connecting argument: one proposed spatial classification constrains several properties that a physical completion must reproduce. The mass comparisons are empirical tests of the chosen assignments; the other rows describe conditional mathematical compatibility with observed property classes. They have different levels of completion, made explicit here rather than counted as equally established predictions.

These constructions reuse geometry already motivated by the mass comparison. That reuse is the explanatory opportunity: an improvement must satisfy several constraints together. It does not turn consequences of shared assumptions into statistically independent discoveries.

Let Measured Decays Constrain the Dynamics

Particles need not be permanently stable. A viable muon or tau model must retain its characteristic structure long enough to reproduce its measured properties, then account for its decay products and energy distribution.

The new decay-tree study follows selected branches and separates internal rest energy from recoil. It provides absolute kinematic energies and cascade constraints. This lets candidate spatial dynamics be checked against actual energy transfer before inferring unknown field amplitudes or stiffnesses.

The Questions That Would Make the Difference

The central open calculation is a common field law that sustains spatial particle patterns and produces their count-scaled frequencies. It must also explain why particular indices occur, derive the sector scales, and recover reciprocal interactions with each pattern retaining its own frequency and polarization. Merely obtaining the right number of modes does not yet make that number an oscillation frequency.

There are focused ways to contribute:

  • Reproduce or challenge the mass comparisons under their stated constraints.
  • Derive a state-selection rule and check which unobserved branches it permits.
  • Connect the geometric orbit and spin constructions to one physical action.
  • Calculate a decay or scattering observable from that action without adjusting each particle or channel separately.

IDWT offers a specific pattern to explain and a set of linked mathematical questions. Independent work on those questions can determine whether the sector structure leads to a physical theory. The detailed case for investigation is the next reading step.
