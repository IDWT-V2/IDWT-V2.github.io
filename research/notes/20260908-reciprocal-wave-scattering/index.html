Reciprocal scattering of spatial modes

Updated September 8, 2026

Summary: A solved conservative wave model turns a two-sided spatial overlap into reflection and transmission, separating protected polarization mismatch from narrow resonances and coherent transparency.

From an allowed overlap to an actual encounter

A nonzero geometric vertex does not specify how much of an incident wave scatters. The target responds, and that response changes the wave which drives it. This note solves that reciprocal problem in a conservative mode benchmark, including both incident polarizations and all retained target shape modes.

The variables are modes of the one field on the nested macroscopic spatial manifold. The propagating modes represent a chosen physical propagation direction, with their bound transverse profiles already included in their normalization. The target variables represent localized spatial changes of the same field. They are not additional mediator particles or additional physical spatial dimensions.

The model is conditional on a self-adjoint first-order mode reduction. A general Dirac linearization retains the ordinary and conjugate blocks in Dirac source linearization; reducing it to the form below requires justification. The lattice dispersion below is an exact solvable benchmark, not a proposed photon dispersion law.

The exact expression remains (9) with its energy-dependent velocity. A broad incident spectrum can therefore scatter weakly even when a finely tuned monochromatic wave scatters strongly.

Consequence for the coupling mechanism

Shape and polarization enter the spatial vertex. Reciprocal field dynamics then determine its resonant enhancement, interference and outgoing flux. An exact rotation-neutral source can close a specified vertex entirely. A small but nonzero overlap instead requires a bandwidth and spectrum calculation before it can explain rare scattering.

To apply this result across the particle hierarchy, derive VVV, HbH_bHb​, the continuum dispersion and the background preparation from the same field action. The higher spatial geometry may determine the full vertex; the two photon polarizations specify the incident channel on which it acts.
