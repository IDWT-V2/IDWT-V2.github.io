2. Why the count depends so strongly on depth

S(n,d)S(n,d)S(n,d) is a theorem of combinatorics. It has several equivalent forms:

S(n,d)=∑k=0n−1(k+d−1d−1)=#{(a0,…,ad)∈Nd+1:∑r=0dar=n−1}. S(n,d) =\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{k+d-1}{d-1} =\#\left\{(a_0,\ldots,a_d)\in\mathbb N^{d+1}: \sum_{r=0}^{d}a_r=n-1\right\}. S(n,d)=k=0∑n−1​(d−1k+d−1​)=#{(a0​,…,ad​)∈Nd+1:r=0∑d​ar​=n−1}.

Thus it is simultaneously

  • the cumulative degeneracy through level n−1n-1n−1 of ddd independent bosonic oscillator directions;
  • the number of lattice points in the standard ddd-simplex dilated by n−1n-1n−1;
  • the dimension of the degree-(n−1)(n-1)(n−1) symmetric power of a (d+1)(d+1)(d+1)-component vector space.

The hockey-stick and Pascal identities are exact:

S(n,d)=∑k=0n−1S(k+1,d−1), S(n,d)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}S(k+1,d-1), S(n,d)=k=0∑n−1​S(k+1,d−1),

Changing ddd changes the shape and polynomial degree of a family spectrum. A common μd\mu_dμd​ changes its vertical scale. Widely separated mode indices are especially powerful depth discriminators.
